\begin{abstract}
Multi-second vehicle trajectory prediction supports collision mitigation and signal control in Cooperative Intelligent Transportation Systems (C-ITS), but centralizing coordinates raises privacy and bandwidth concerns. Federated Learning (FL) across Roadside Units (RSUs) coordinates training without raw telemetry centralization, yet freeway, arterial, and downtown corridors exhibit geographically non-identically distributed (non-IID) kinematics. We benchmark and audit federated and personalized trajectory prediction across three functional zones of the Next Generation Simulation (NGSIM) corpus using contiguous trajectory assembly, kinematic physics baselines, matched training budgets, and five repeated seeds. Over five random seeds, Centralized training achieved a pooled Average Displacement Error (ADE) of 0.835 $\pm$ 0.028\,m, while FedAvg reached 0.887 $\pm$ 0.048\,m (12.2\% normalized ADE) and FedPer reached 0.930 $\pm$ 0.058\,m, outperforming Constant Velocity (0.990\,m) and Constant Acceleration (1.329\,m) baselines. Dedicated local models achieved 0.880 $\pm$ 0.048\,m in their home zone, confirming local specialization remains competitive, but degraded by up to 5.04-fold out-of-domain. Gradient inversion audits on single victim windows ($|B|=1$) demonstrate that unperturbed gradients allow coordinate reconstruction (0.437\,m error), whereas Gaussian noise perturbation ($\sigma=1.0$) empirically increases reconstruction error to 5.198\,m. Separately, formal multi-round batch DP-SGD accounting ($|B|=128, \delta=10^{-5}$) bounds the worst-case privacy budget at $\epsilon \le 8.51$ (per-client range $2.46$--$8.51$; $\epsilon \le 2.80$ at $\sigma=2.0$). Membership inference yielded near-chance detection (AUC 0.408, 51.0\% accuracy). These findings establish empirical trade-offs between accuracy, edge bandwidth, and privacy accounting across heterogeneous corridors.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ooperative} Intelligent Transportation Systems (C-ITS) and connected vehicular networks constitute critical infrastructure in contemporary smart cities, where continuous foresight of vehicular motion is indispensable for public safety and traffic efficiency~\cite{zhao2026digitaltwin}. Multi-second vehicle trajectory prediction enables autonomous vehicle navigation systems, connected roadside units, and traffic management centers to anticipate unsafe lane maneuvers, mitigate intersection collisions, and optimize adaptive traffic signal controllers~\cite{alhuthaifi2024fedagat,meese2024adaptive}. Modern deep sequence architectures, such as spatial-temporal graph networks and recurrent neural networks, excel at modeling complex multi-agent kinematics when supplied with continuous motion telemetry~\cite{shen2024decentralized}.

Conventionally, training these predictive models has relied upon centralizing dense vehicular telemetry, including geographic coordinates, heading angles, velocities, and acceleration profiles, within centralized municipal data centers~\cite{ni2024federated}. However, continuous location streaming exposes commuter itineraries to privacy violations under municipal data protection statutes such as GDPR~\cite{han2022federated,crivellari2026federated}, while consuming wireless cellular backhaul bandwidth and exposing monolithic databases to systemic breaches~\cite{zhang2022communication,zafar2024federated,reddy2026zero}. To address these centralization risks, Federated Learning (FL) has emerged as a decentralized paradigm, coordinating model training across distributed Roadside Units (RSUs) by exchanging parameterized gradient updates rather than raw vehicular coordinates~\cite{mcmahan2017communication,li2020federated,liu2025trajectory,li2025fedsectp}.

Nevertheless, deploying canonical federated learning within real-world smart cities encounters an unresolved obstacle: geographic non-identically and independently distributed (non-IID) mobility distributions across functional urban roadway sectors~\cite{fhwa2006ngsim}. In metropolitan transportation networks, vehicular kinematics are strongly shaped by local road geometry, speed regulations, and traffic control topology. For instance, high-speed freeway corridors (e.g., US-101~\cite{fhwa2006ngsim}) are characterized by continuous high longitudinal velocities and gradual lane changes. In contrast, signalized arterial corridors (e.g., Lankershim Boulevard~\cite{fhwa2006ngsim}) and downtown commercial street networks (e.g., Peachtree Street~\cite{fhwa2006ngsim}) are dominated by frequent deceleration, stop-and-go queues, pedestrian interactions, and sharp perpendicular turns. When standard global federated aggregation algorithms enforce a single uniform parameter vector across such divergent kinematic environments, the resulting aggregation can produce client drift, gradient conflict, and elevated regional performance disparity~\cite{lee2024layerwise}. Furthermore, while federated learning circumvents raw data transmissions, adversarial studies demonstrate that unperturbed model parameter updates remain vulnerable to gradient inversion and membership inference attacks~\cite{zhu2019deep,zhao2020idlg,shokri2017membership}. The trade-offs between predictive accuracy, regional spatial fairness, wireless communication bandwidth, and privacy across heterogeneous urban edge deployments have not been comprehensively established in existing literature.

The primary objective of this study is to benchmark and audit federated and personalized federated trajectory prediction across three heterogeneous NGSIM zones, comparing them with models trained on each zone alone, and reporting which commonly claimed benefits of federation (accuracy, disparity, communication, and privacy) the empirical findings support, with principal contributions summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Quantification of Geographic Non-IID Mobility Divergence:} A measurement of kinematic distribution shifts between freeway, arterial, and downtown corridors of the Next Generation Simulation (NGSIM) corpus~\cite{fhwa2006ngsim}, using the mean pairwise Jensen-Shannon divergence over velocity, acceleration, and longitudinal displacement ($\overline{\mathrm{JSD}} = 0.1088$) and gradient cosine distance ($d_{\cos} = 0.2790$, measured at initialization).
    \item \textbf{Three-Zone Benchmark with Matched Budgets and Physical Baselines:} A multi-seed benchmark across five random seeds comparing centralized, isolated, FedAvg, FedProx, pFedMe, and FedPer~\cite{arivazhagan2019federated} against Constant Velocity (0.990\,m ADE) and Constant Acceleration (1.329\,m ADE) physics baselines under matched 12-epoch/round budgets and contiguous trajectory assembly. Centralized pooled training achieved a pooled ADE of 0.835 $\pm$ 0.028\,m, while FedAvg reached 0.887 $\pm$ 0.048\,m (12.2\% normalized ADE) and FedPer reached 0.930 $\pm$ 0.058\,m. Isolated own-zone models achieved 0.880 $\pm$ 0.048\,m, but isolated silos degraded by up to 5.04-fold (5.028\,m ADE) when deployed on foreign corridors, indicating that federated representation sharing mitigates substantial out-of-distribution transfer degradation.
    \item \textbf{Gradient Inversion Auditing and Differential Privacy Accounting:} An empirical evaluation of trajectory reconstruction vulnerability via Deep Leakage from Gradients (DLG)~\cite{zhu2019deep} and Membership Inference Attacks (MIA)~\cite{shokri2017membership}, strictly decoupled from formal privacy accounting. On single victim windows ($|B|=1$), unprotected FedAvg exchanges leak coordinates (0.437\,m position error), and withholding personalization heads alone does not prevent reconstruction (0.296\,m error), while Gaussian perturbation ($\sigma=1.0$) degrades reconstruction beyond vehicle lane widths (5.198\,m error). Separately, multi-round batch DP-SGD with a R\'enyi Differential Privacy moments accountant ($|B|=128, \delta=10^{-5}$) provides calibrated analytical bounds (worst-case $\epsilon \le 8.51$ at $\sigma=1.0$, per-client range $2.46$--$8.51$; $\epsilon \le 2.80$ at $\sigma=2.0$, range $0.82$--$2.80$; $\epsilon \le 38.74$ at $\sigma=0.5$), while membership inference remains at near-chance discriminability (AUC 0.408, 51.00\% accuracy).
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 describes the proposed methodology. Section 4 presents the experimental results. Section 5 discusses the findings and limitations, and Section 6 concludes.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature}

\subsection{Deep Sequential Models for Vehicle Trajectory Forecasting}
Vehicle trajectory forecasting has evolved from classical physical kinematic models and social force formulations toward deep neural sequence architectures. Recurrent neural networks, specifically Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Units (GRU), provide flexible temporal abstractions capable of modeling non-linear vehicle acceleration and velocity transitions over multi-second horizons~\cite{crivellari2026federated,mundada2026federated}. Recent investigations have incorporated Bahdanau attention mechanisms, spatial-temporal graph neural networks, and Transformer encoders to model multi-vehicle interdependencies and road geometry constraints~\cite{zhao2026digitaltwin,qi2023fedagcn}. Nevertheless, the empirical efficacy of these deep neural networks remains contingent upon harvesting extensive datasets across large driving corpora. In municipal transportation networks, centralizing raw coordinate streams from thousands of connected vehicles and roadside sensors incurs communication overhead and exposes sensitive travel itineraries, motivating the need for decentralized edge learning paradigms~\cite{ni2024federated,zhang2022communication}.

\subsection{Federated Learning in Cooperative Intelligent Transportation}
Federated learning allows distributed edge devices to cooperatively optimize machine learning models while retaining raw sensory observations on local hardware~\cite{mcmahan2017communication}. Within Cooperative Intelligent Transportation Systems, federated learning has been investigated for intersection traffic signal management, decentralized speed forecasting, and connected vehicle collision warning~\cite{meese2024adaptive,liu2025trajectory}. Li et al.~\cite{li2024vsfl} designed a validity-aware semi-asynchronous federated framework to accommodate intermittent vehicular backhaul disconnections. Similarly, Reddy and Muralidhar~\cite{reddy2026zero} introduced zero-knowledge verifiable federated learning on permissioned blockchains to audit vehicular model parameter integrity. While these studies illustrate the communication and sovereignty advantages of federated edge computing, the majority evaluate models under artificially uniform or mildly synthetic data distributions, neglecting the structural kinematic shifts inherent to distinct real-world urban road facilities.

\subsection{Non-IID Heterogeneity and Personalized Federated Learning}
Statistical data heterogeneity represents one of the primary theoretical and operational bottlenecks in federated optimization~\cite{li2020federated}. When data distributions across edge clients diverge, localized gradient updates guide client models toward contradictory regional parameter minima, causing client drift and degrading global aggregation stability~\cite{mcmahan2017communication,li2020federated}. To mitigate client drift, Li et al.~\cite{li2020federated} introduced FedProx, which introduces an explicit proximal regularization penalty to bound local updates to the global consensus. However, enforcing strict model parameter consensus across fundamentally distinct operational environments can produce suboptimal predictive performance for participating clients.

To reconcile global collaborative training with localized environmental requirements, Personalized Federated Learning (PFL) has gained significant traction. Arivazhagan et al.~\cite{arivazhagan2019federated} proposed FedPer, which decouples deep neural networks into shared feature representation backbones and private localized output projection heads. Dinh et al.~\cite{dinh2020personalized} formulated pFedMe, framing personalized federated learning as a bi-level optimization problem regulated by Moreau envelopes to balance local specialization against global consensus. Lee et al.~\cite{lee2024layerwise} implemented layer-wise personalized parameter updates. Despite these theoretical advances, systematic evaluations analyzing how personalized federated architectures compress spatial performance disparity and defend against continuous coordinate leakage across diverse urban roadside environments remain absent.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Although existing literature demonstrates the viability of federated computing for traffic forecasting, research gaps persist at the intersection of geographic non-IID kinematic distributions, regional spatial equity, and continuous trajectory privacy auditing. Existing studies either assume synthetic client distributions, overlook regional error disparities across high-speed and urban road corridors, or assume that gradient exchanges are inherently secure without conducting empirical gradient reconstruction audits. \section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}
We consider a smart city Cooperative Intelligent Transportation System comprising $K$ distributed Roadside Units (RSUs), denoted by $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, c_2, \dots, c_K\}$, coordinated by a central RSU Gateway Server. Each RSU $c_k$ is situated at a distinct urban road facility (e.g., freeway corridor, signalized arterial, downtown intersection) and collects a localized dataset $\mathcal{D}_k = \{(X_i^{(k)}, Y_i^{(k)})\}_{i=1}^{N_k}$, where $N_k = |\mathcal{D}_k|$ denotes the number of trajectory records harvested by RSU $k$. The total number of trajectory samples across the metropolitan region is $N = \sum_{k=1}^K N_k$. In this study $K = 3$, so the experiments are a three-zone transfer study rather than a large-scale federation.

Let $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$ denote the historical observation horizon and $T_{\mathrm{pred}}$ denote the forecasted prediction horizon. Trajectory coordinates are sampled at a discrete frequency of $f_s = 2$\,Hz (temporal interval $\Delta t = 0.5$\,s)~\cite{fhwa2006ngsim}. The observed historical sequence of vehicle $i$ at RSU $k$ over discrete timesteps $t \in \{-T_{\mathrm{obs}}+1, \dots, -1, 0\}$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
X_i^{(k)} = [s_{-T_{\mathrm{obs}}+1}^{(i)}, \dots, s_{-1}^{(i)}, s_0^{(i)}]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\mathrm{obs}} \times d_{\mathrm{in}}},
\label{eq:input_sequence}
\end{equation}
where each kinematic state vector $s_t^{(i)}$ is defined in a four-dimensional feature space ($d_{\mathrm{in}} = 4$):
\begin{equation}
s_t^{(i)} = [\Delta x_t^{(i)}, \Delta y_t^{(i)}, v_t^{(i)}, a_t^{(i)}]^\top.
\label{eq:state_vector}
\end{equation}
Here, $\Delta x_t^{(i)} = x_t^{(i)} - x_0^{(i)}$ and $\Delta y_t^{(i)} = y_t^{(i)} - y_0^{(i)}$ represent lateral (NGSIM \texttt{Local\_X}) and longitudinal (\texttt{Local\_Y}) coordinates expressed relative to the vehicle's position at reference observation timestep $t=0$, providing translation invariance across map coordinate systems. The scalar quantities $v_t^{(i)}$ and $a_t^{(i)}$ denote instantaneous speed and acceleration (\texttt{v\_Vel}, \texttt{v\_Acc}), respectively. All four features and the targets are standardized with the mean and standard deviation of the pooled training windows of all clients; predictions are de-standardized to feet and multiplied by 0.3048 to obtain metres before any metric is computed.

The ground-truth future trajectory sequence to be forecasted over future timesteps $t \in \{1, 2, \dots, T_{\mathrm{pred}}\}$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
Y_i^{(k)} = [p_1^{(i)}, p_2^{(i)}, \dots, p_{T_{\mathrm{pred}}}^{(i)}]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\mathrm{pred}} \times d_{\mathrm{out}}},
\label{eq:target_sequence}
\end{equation}
where $p_t^{(i)} = [\Delta x_t^{(i)}, \Delta y_t^{(i)}]^\top$ represents future relative coordinate offsets ($d_{\mathrm{out}} = 2$). In our system formulation, $T_{\mathrm{obs}} = 6$ frames ($3.0$\,s history) and $T_{\mathrm{pred}} = 6$ frames ($3.0$\,s future horizon). While widely cited NGSIM benchmarks frequently adopt an asymmetric 3\,s observation / 5\,s prediction horizon (evaluating 10 future steps), this study intentionally benchmarks a symmetric 3.0\,s observation / 3.0\,s prediction horizon (6 frames each at 2\,Hz) to evaluate balanced encoder-decoder latent representations across functional corridors without temporal attenuation confounding early-round federated aggregation. Consequently, the quantitative errors reported herein should be assessed within this dedicated 6-second window formulation rather than cross-compared directly against 5-second horizon studies, with multi-second extension to 5.0\,s horizons designated for future work.

The global federated objective across all $K$ roadside units is to optimize a neural parameter vector $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ minimizing the composite empirical risk:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d} \mathcal{F}(\theta) = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{N_k}{N} \mathcal{F}_k(\theta),
\label{eq:fl_objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}_k(\theta)$ is the localized empirical loss computed over local dataset $\mathcal{D}_k$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_k(\theta) = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{i=1}^{N_k} \mathcal{L}(f(X_i^{(k)}; \theta), Y_i^{(k)}).
\label{eq:local_loss}
\end{equation}
The sequence loss function $\mathcal{L}(\cdot)$ is formulated as the sequence Mean Squared Error (MSE):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\hat{Y}_i^{(k)}, Y_i^{(k)}) = \frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \|\hat{p}_t^{(i)} - p_t^{(i)}\|_2^2.
\label{eq:mse_loss}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Data and Materials}
\label{sec:data_materials}
We utilize real-world vehicular motion telemetry from the Next Generation Simulation (NGSIM) program published by the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA)~\cite{fhwa2006ngsim}. The benchmark uses a slice of 999,999 trajectory records partitioned across three geographically distinct functional road facilities acting as localized edge RSU clients. To ensure strict data reproducibility and eliminate cross-session track corruption, data preparation executes the following exact sequence of operations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ingestion and Slice Selection:} The combined NGSIM vehicle trajectory export is ingested in sequential chunk order, parsing columns \texttt{Vehicle\_ID}, \texttt{Frame\_ID}, \texttt{Global\_Time}, \texttt{Local\_X}, \texttt{Local\_Y}, \texttt{v\_Vel}, \texttt{v\_Acc}, and \texttt{Location}. Numerical values are sanitized, and rows with missing or malformed fields are discarded. Exactly the first 333,333 valid records in file order are retained for each of the three locations (\texttt{us-101}, \texttt{lankershim}, and \texttt{peachtree}), yielding an exact 999,999-record slice without time-window or lane-specific filtering (the I-80 freeway dataset is excluded).
    \item \textbf{Chronological Sorting and Deduplication:} Within each location, duplicate records sharing identical \texttt{[Vehicle\_ID, Global\_Time, Frame\_ID]} tuples are dropped, and records are sorted in ascending order of \texttt{[Vehicle\_ID, Global\_Time, Frame\_ID]}.
    \item \textbf{Composite-Key Contiguity and Session Segmentation:} Because raw NGSIM records originate from multiple 15-minute recording periods where vehicle identifiers are recycled or tracking undergoes occlusion, contiguous trajectory segments are segmented chronologically. A new physical segment boundary is instantiated whenever $\texttt{Vehicle\_ID}_t \ne \texttt{Vehicle\_ID}_{t-1}$, $\texttt{Frame\_ID}_t \ne \texttt{Frame\_ID}_{t-1} + 1$, or $\texttt{Global\_Time}_t \ne \texttt{Global\_Time}_{t-1} + 100$\,ms (at native 10\,Hz). Each continuous block receives a unique \texttt{segment\_id}.
    \item \textbf{Duration Filtering and Vehicle Entity Sampling:} Segments containing fewer than 60 raw consecutive frames (6.0\,s at 10\,Hz) are discarded to ensure sufficient temporal support for observation and prediction horizons. Unique vehicle identifiers within valid segments are shuffled using fixed pseudo-random seed 42, retaining at most 600 representative vehicles per location to preserve domain balance.
    \item \textbf{Disjoint Vehicle-Level Splitting:} Unique vehicle identifiers within each location are partitioned into disjoint sets using a target 70\% training, 15\% validation, and 15\% testing ratio under fixed seed 42. Partitioning strictly at the vehicle entity level guarantees zero vehicle overlap across splits, producing exact partition window counts (US-101: 1,351 training, 297 validation, 312 testing; Lankershim: 7,369 training, 1,660 validation, 1,680 testing; Peachtree: 11,193 training, 2,338 validation, 2,507 testing).
    \item \textbf{Temporal Downsampling and Window Extraction:} Surviving contiguous segments are downsampled by a factor of 5 (from 10\,Hz to 2\,Hz, $\Delta t = 0.5$\,s). Trajectory sequences of total length $T_{\mathrm{obs}} + T_{\mathrm{pred}} = 6 + 6 = 12$ steps (6.0\,s) are extracted with a sliding stride of 3 steps (1.5\,s). Coordinates are standardized to relative displacement $\Delta p_t = p_t - p_0$ from the initial observation point.
\end{enumerate}

The resulting client domain datasets are partitioned into disjoint 70\% training, 15\% validation, and 15\% testing splits (yielding an exact pooled test suite of 4,499 windows evaluated across all five random seeds, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_overview}a):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Client 0 (RSU-1: US-101 Highway):} Multi-lane interstate freeway corridor in Los Angeles, California ($1,351$ training, $297$ validation, and $312$ test windows; mean speed $13.73 \pm 8.49$\,ft/s [$4.18 \pm 2.59$\,m/s]; mean acceleration $-0.35 \pm 4.25$\,ft/s$^2$ [$-0.11 \pm 1.30$\,m/s$^2$]; mean path length over the observation window $123.57 \pm 79.28$\,ft [$37.66 \pm 24.16$\,m]). Characterized by high longitudinal velocities and gradual lane merges.
    \item \textbf{Client 1 (RSU-2: Lankershim Boulevard):} Signalized commercial arterial corridor in Los Angeles, California ($7,369$ training, $1,660$ validation, and $1,680$ test windows; mean speed $5.68 \pm 9.64$\,ft/s [$1.73 \pm 2.94$\,m/s]; mean acceleration $-0.22 \pm 3.53$\,ft/s$^2$ [$-0.07 \pm 1.08$\,m/s$^2$]; mean path length over the observation window $22.60 \pm 43.56$\,ft [$6.89 \pm 13.28$\,m]). Governed by signal phases, stop-and-go queuing, and lane-selection maneuvers.
    \item \textbf{Client 2 (RSU-3: Peachtree Street):} Dense downtown commercial arterial grid in Atlanta, Georgia ($11,193$ training, $2,338$ validation, and $2,507$ test windows; mean speed $9.31 \pm 14.11$\,ft/s [$2.84 \pm 4.30$\,m/s]; mean acceleration $-0.09 \pm 3.80$\,ft/s$^2$ [$-0.03 \pm 1.16$\,m/s$^2$]; mean path length over the observation window $28.99 \pm 57.29$\,ft [$8.84 \pm 17.46$\,m]). Characterized by tight corner turns, low speeds, and roadside parking friction.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Pipeline verification and outlier audit.} A previous preliminary version of this pipeline grouped records by \texttt{Vehicle\_ID} alone without checking frame continuity across 15-minute recording periods, causing disparate vehicle tracks to be stitched into non-physical paths with displacement spikes. We resolved this defect by incorporating composite key segmentation and strict 100\,ms contiguity verification prior to downsampling on the combined 999,999-record slice from \texttt{export.csv}. The corrected dataset eliminates track stitching, yielding maximum 0.5\,s step displacements: US-101 (6.82\,m), Lankershim (9.08\,m), and Peachtree (14.00\,m). An explicit audit of the 14.00\,m maximum displacement on Peachtree revealed it at training window index 10712, where consecutive steps jump from $[-1.78, -16.50]$\,ft to $[0.32, -62.38]$\,ft ($\Delta y = 45.88$\,ft in 0.5\,s, equivalent to 28.0\,m/s or 62.6\,mph), following a preceding backward tracking oscillation of 13.6\,ft. This artifact reflects known optical video tracking and occlusion jitter inherent to the raw FHWA video transcription at street intersections. Importantly, composite key contiguity verification prevents cross-session track stitching across 15-minute recording periods, but intentionally preserves within-session raw tracking jitter to avoid artificial data sanitization. Across the full Peachtree partition, 99.9\% of 0.5\,s displacements remain below 8.34\,m (mean step displacement 1.42\,m); retaining this isolated tracking artifact rather than post-hoc manual filtering preserves empirical data integrity. A documented sensitivity analysis demonstrates that retraining on the remaining 11,192 training windows under seed 42 with the test partition held fixed at 2,507 windows shifts global pooled test ADE by less than 0.001\,m (from 0.880\,m to 0.879\,m for isolated own-zone models), confirming that benchmark conclusions are insensitive to this raw sensor tracking artifact. All benchmark experiments in Section~\ref{sec:results} are evaluated on these clean, verified contiguous sequences.

\subsubsection{Deep Sequence-to-Sequence LSTM Backbone}
To model continuous trajectory sequences, each RSU deploys an encoder-decoder LSTM network. All reported results were obtained with this model; an attention variant present in the code base was not used in any experiment.
\begin{align}
e_t &= \mathrm{Dropout}(\mathrm{ReLU}(W_{\mathrm{in}} s_t + b_{\mathrm{in}})), \label{eq:embedding} \\
h_t^{\mathrm{enc}}, c_t^{\mathrm{enc}} &= \mathrm{LSTM}_{\mathrm{enc}}(e_t, (h_{t-1}^{\mathrm{enc}}, c_{t-1}^{\mathrm{enc}})), \label{eq:enc_lstm}
\end{align}
where $W_{\mathrm{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{embed}} \times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ and $b_{\mathrm{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{embed}}}$ denote input projection weights and bias ($d_{\mathrm{embed}} = 64$), $\mathrm{LSTM}_{\mathrm{enc}}$ has two layers, $d_h = 64$ represents the hidden state dimensionality, and the dropout rate is 0.2. The decoder is a two-layer LSTM initialized with the final encoder states of both layers, $(h_0^{\mathrm{dec}}, c_0^{\mathrm{dec}}) = (h_{T_{\mathrm{obs}}}^{\mathrm{enc}}, c_{T_{\mathrm{obs}}}^{\mathrm{enc}})$. Its input at every future step is the final top-layer encoder state, so decoding is not autoregressive:
\begin{align}
h_t^{\mathrm{dec}}, c_t^{\mathrm{dec}} &= \mathrm{LSTM}_{\mathrm{dec}}(h_{T_{\mathrm{obs}}}^{\mathrm{enc}}, (h_{t-1}^{\mathrm{dec}}, c_{t-1}^{\mathrm{dec}})), \label{eq:dec_lstm} \\
\hat{p}_t &= W_2 \mathrm{ReLU}(W_1 h_t^{\mathrm{dec}} + b_1) + b_2, \label{eq:mlp_projection}
\end{align}
where $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{mlp}} \times d_h}$, $b_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{mlp}}}$, $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times d_{\mathrm{mlp}}}$, and $b_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}$ ($d_{\mathrm{mlp}} = 32$, $d_{\mathrm{out}} = 2$). The parameter count is as follows: input embedding $4 \cdot 64 + 64 = 320$; each LSTM layer with 64 inputs and 64 hidden units $4(64 \cdot 64 + 64 \cdot 64 + 2 \cdot 64) = 33,280$, giving $66,560$ for the encoder and $66,560$ for the decoder; output head $64 \cdot 32 + 32 + 32 \cdot 2 + 2 = 2,146$. The full network comprises $135,586$ trainable parameters ($529.63$\,KB in float32).

\subsubsection{Quantification of Geographic Non-IID Mobility Divergence}
To quantify kinematic non-IID shifts across RSU domains $\mathcal{D}_i$ and $\mathcal{D}_j$, we compute divergence metrics over the standardized speed, acceleration, and longitudinal displacement features of the training windows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence:} Computed over $B=50$ histogram bins on a common range with smoothing factor $\varepsilon = 10^{-10}$:
    \begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_i \parallel P_j) = \sum_{b=1}^B P_i(b) \ln \left( \frac{P_i(b) + \varepsilon}{P_j(b) + \varepsilon} \right).
    \label{eq:kl_div}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Jensen-Shannon (JS) Divergence:} Symmetric and bounded metric evaluating divergence against midpoint $M = \frac{1}{2}(P_i + P_j)$:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathrm{JSD}(P_i \parallel P_j) = \frac{1}{2} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_i \parallel M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_j \parallel M).
    \label{eq:jsd}
    \end{equation}
    The aggregate regional heterogeneity score is the mean over the three kinematic features $\mathcal{F} = \{v, a, \Delta y\}$ (instantaneous speed, acceleration, and longitudinal displacement) and zone pairs:
    \begin{equation}
    \overline{\mathrm{JSD}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|} \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \frac{2}{K(K-1)} \sum_{1 \le i < j \le K} \mathrm{JSD}(P_i^{(f)} \parallel P_j^{(f)}).
    \label{eq:mean_jsd}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Parameter-Space Gradient Divergence:} Evaluates directional conflicts between client gradient vectors $g_i, g_j$ via cosine distance:
    \begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{cos}}(g_i, g_j) = 1 - \frac{\langle g_i, g_j \rangle}{\|g_i\|_2 \|g_j\|_2}.
    \label{eq:cos_div}
    \end{equation}
    The reported $d_{\cos}$ is the mean over client pairs, computed from one batch of 64 training windows per client on a freshly initialized model.
\end{itemize}
Pairwise KL divergences and Wasserstein-1 distances were also computed during the experiments but were not retained in the result files, so only $\overline{\mathrm{JSD}}$ and $d_{\cos}$ are reported.

\subsubsection{Personalized Federated Learning via FedPer}
To reduce client drift while capturing shared temporal dynamics, we implement Personalized Federated Learning via FedPer~\cite{arivazhagan2019federated}. The model parameter vector is partitioned into:
\begin{equation}
\theta = [w_{\mathrm{base}}, w_{\mathrm{head}}],
\label{eq:param_split}
\end{equation}
where $w_{\mathrm{base}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{base}}}$ comprises the shared spatial-temporal representation backbone ($133,440$ parameters, $98.42\%$ of model weights, $521.25$\,KiB), consisting of the input embedding, the encoder LSTM, and the decoder LSTM. The personalized component $w_{\mathrm{head}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{head}}}$ comprises the final regression projection layers ($2,146$ parameters, $1.58\%$ of model weights, $8.38$\,KiB; combined parameter volume $529.63$\,KiB).

In each communication round, only the base parameters $w_{\mathrm{base}}$ are uploaded to and aggregated by the gateway server:
\begin{equation}
w_{\mathrm{base}}^{t+1} = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{N_k}{N} w_{\mathrm{base}, k}^{t+1}.
\label{eq:base_aggregation}
\end{equation}
The personalized head $w_{\mathrm{head}, k}$ is retained on-device at RSU $k$ and updated exclusively via local gradient descent on $\mathcal{D}_k$. The execution flow of FedPer is detailed in Algorithm~\ref{alg:fedper}.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Personalized Federated Learning via FedPer for RSU Networks}
\label{alg:fedper}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE RSUs $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, \dots, c_K\}$ with private datasets $\mathcal{D}_k$, communication rounds $R$, local epochs $E$, learning rate $\eta$.
\ENSURE Global base model $w_{\mathrm{base}}^R$, localized client heads $\{w_{\mathrm{head}, k}^R\}_{k=1}^K$.
\STATE \textbf{Server initializes} shared base parameters $w_{\mathrm{base}}^0$.
\FOR{round $t = 0, 1, \dots, R-1$}
    \FOR{each RSU client $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ \textbf{in parallel}}
        \STATE Download global base parameters: $w_{\mathrm{base}, k}^{t, 0} \leftarrow w_{\mathrm{base}}^t$.
        \STATE Retain local private head: $w_{\mathrm{head}, k}^{t, 0} \leftarrow w_{\mathrm{head}, k}^t$.
        \FOR{epoch $e = 1, \dots, E$}
            \FOR{mini-batch $(X, Y) \sim \mathcal{D}_k$}
                \STATE $\hat{Y} \leftarrow f(X; [w_{\mathrm{base}, k}^{t}, w_{\mathrm{head}, k}^{t}])$
                \STATE $\mathcal{L} \leftarrow \frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \sum_{\tau=1}^{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \|\hat{p}_\tau - p_\tau\|_2^2$
                \STATE $[w_{\mathrm{base}, k}^{t}, w_{\mathrm{head}, k}^{t}] \leftarrow [w_{\mathrm{base}, k}^{t}, w_{\mathrm{head}, k}^{t}] - \eta \nabla \mathcal{L}$
            \ENDFOR
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE Upload updated base parameters $w_{\mathrm{base}, k}^{t+1}$ to Server.
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE \textbf{Server aggregates}: $w_{\mathrm{base}}^{t+1} \leftarrow \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{N_k}{N} w_{\mathrm{base}, k}^{t+1}$.
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
Note that while the localized update step in Algorithm~\ref{alg:fedper} is written schematically as standard gradient descent with learning rate $\eta$, the empirical implementation executes the Adam optimizer ($\eta = 0.002, \beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.999$, weight decay $10^{-5}$) for adaptive moment-based parameter updates across batches.

\subsubsection{Continuous Gradient Inversion Audit and Differential Privacy Accounting}
To audit parameter privacy against honest-but-curious servers or wireless eavesdroppers, we adapt Deep Leakage from Gradients (DLG)~\cite{zhu2019deep} and Improved DLG (iDLG)~\cite{zhao2020idlg} to continuous multi-variate trajectory regression. We evaluate gradient inversion under worst-case privacy leakage conditions where the adversary intercepts parameter gradients computed on a single trajectory observation window ($|B|=1$) at the initial training round ($t=0$, before parameter dispersion across clients). The attack is evaluated across 10 independent victim trajectory windows sampled from Client 0's training partition. An adversary observing client parameter gradients $g_v = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(f(X_v; \theta), Y_v)$ synthesizes dummy input kinematics $\hat{X}$ and dummy trajectory targets $\hat{Y}$ by minimizing:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\hat{X}, \hat{Y}} \sum_{l=1}^L \|\nabla_{\theta_l} \mathcal{L}(f(\hat{X}; \theta), \hat{Y}) - g_{v, l}\|_2^2 + \lambda_{\mathrm{TV}} \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\hat{X}),
\label{eq:dlg_loss}
\end{equation}
where Total Variation (TV) regularization $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\hat{X}) = \sum_{t=2}^{T_{\mathrm{obs}}} \|\hat{X}_{t, 1:2} - \hat{X}_{t-1, 1:2}\|_2^2$ preserves motion continuity ($\lambda_{\mathrm{TV}} = 10^{-4}$). The attack executes over 150 iterations using the Adam optimizer ($\eta=0.1$). For FedAvg, the adversary matches gradients across all model layers. For FedPer, the adversary at round 0 is assumed to know the initial architecture and initialization weights $w_{\mathrm{head}}^0$; because the client withholds head gradient updates, the adversary matches gradients exclusively against the shared base backbone ($133,440$ parameters, 98.42\% of model weights). We explicitly acknowledge that multi-round gradient inversion under accumulated unknown local head drift was not evaluated. Reconstruction errors are converted to physical metres via de-standardization and imperial-to-metric conversion ($1\,\text{ft} = 0.3048\,\text{m}$).

To counter gradient inversion and bound privacy leakage, we incorporate Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD)~\cite{abadi2016deep} via per-sample gradient clipping and calibrated Gaussian perturbation. For each mini-batch $B \subset \mathcal{D}_k$ of size $|B|=128$, per-sample gradients $g_i = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(f(X_i; \theta), Y_i)$ are clipped to a maximum Euclidean norm threshold $C$:
\begin{equation}
\bar{g}_i = \frac{g_i}{\max\left(1, \, \frac{\|g_i\|_2}{C}\right)}, \quad \forall i \in B.
\label{eq:dp_clip}
\end{equation}
The privatized batch gradient update $\tilde{g}$ transmitted by client $k$ is formed by aggregating clipped gradients and adding spherical Gaussian perturbation:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{g} = \frac{1}{|B|} \left( \sum_{i \in B} \bar{g}_i + \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I\right) \right),
\label{eq:dp_sgd}
\end{equation}
where $C = 1.0$ is the clipping bound and $\sigma \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$ denotes the noise multiplier.

\textit{Privacy Unit and Accountant Parameters.} The differential privacy guarantee applies at the level of individual 3-second trajectory observation windows (window-level DP), bounding the information leakage regarding any single continuous kinematic observation window recorded by an RSU. Because consecutive sliding windows from the same vehicle share overlapping frames, extending this guarantee to vehicle-level or trip-level DP across full commuter journeys would scale privacy loss by the number of windows via group privacy composition. Under local batch size $B=128$, local epochs $E=1$, and communication rounds $R=12$, local optimizer steps per client are $T_0 = 12 \times 11 = 132$, $T_1 = 12 \times 58 = 696$, and $T_2 = 12 \times 88 = 1{,}056$ steps, retaining final partial batches without dropping records. Privacy parameters $(\epsilon, \delta)$ are computed using the official Opacus 1.6.0 R\'enyi Differential Privacy (RDP) moments accountant~\cite{abadi2016deep} for the subsampled Gaussian mechanism ($C=1.0, \delta=10^{-5}$) under the standard Poisson subsampling approximation with nominal ratio $q_k = B / N_k$: Client 0 ($N_0 = 1{,}351, q_0 \approx 0.0947$), Client 1 ($N_1 = 7{,}369, q_1 \approx 0.0174$), and Client 2 ($N_2 = 11{,}193, q_2 \approx 0.0114$). Because PyTorch DataLoaders retain partial final batches (e.g., 71 windows for US-101), modeling subsampling via the nominal full-batch ratio $q_k = B/N_k$ yields an analytical upper bound on privacy loss under the Poisson subsampling model. The federation-wide differential privacy bound is governed by the worst-case client (Client 0, US-101), attaining $\epsilon \le 8.51$ at $\sigma = 1.0$ ($\epsilon \le 2.80$ at $\sigma = 2.0$, and $\epsilon \le 38.74$ at $\sigma = 0.5$). Evaluating the remaining clients yields $\epsilon \le 3.14$ for Client 1 ($\epsilon \le 1.03$ at $\sigma=2.0$, $\epsilon \le 20.64$ at $\sigma=0.5$) and $\epsilon \le 2.46$ for Client 2 ($\epsilon \le 0.82$ at $\sigma=2.0$, $\epsilon \le 17.50$ at $\sigma=0.5$). An earlier draft reported provisional client bounds of $\epsilon \le 5.46$ ($\sigma=1.0$), $\epsilon \le 2.60$ ($\sigma=2.0$), and $\epsilon \le 12.01$ ($\sigma=0.5$) based on historical pre-filtering sample counts ($N_0=1{,}430, N_1=6{,}094, N_2=7{,}364$) and a simplified quadratic Taylor approximation $\mathrm{RDP}(\alpha) \approx q^2 \alpha / (2\sigma^2)$; recomputing via Opacus 1.6.0 directly on verified contiguous partitions and true step counts provides the verified theoretical privacy bounds reported here. In our gradient inversion auditing experiments, the adversary intercepts separately perturbed gradients computed on individual victim observation windows ($|B|=1$, with per-sample clipping $C=1.0$ and Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I)$ added to the victim gradient), directly isolating the reconstruction defense of the perturbation mechanism itself, rather than reconstructing from full 128-sample mini-batch updates which would inherently further dilute single-trajectory coordinate recovery. Importantly, the RDP bounds $(\epsilon, \delta)$ mathematically describe the iterative multi-round batch DP-SGD federation mechanism ($|B|=128$); they do not constitute a closed-form differential privacy guarantee for the single-window gradient inversion attack ($|B|=1$), which serves as an empirical stress test of coordinate reconstruction defense.

In addition, a Membership Inference Attack (MIA)~\cite{shokri2017membership} is conducted on converged models using a loss-threshold classifier evaluated across 200 member training windows and 200 non-member test windows from Client 0. Member trajectories typically incur lower prediction loss $\mathcal{L}$; prediction scores are defined as negative loss ($s = -\mathcal{L}$) such that lower loss maps to higher membership likelihood. Under this standard negative-loss scoring orientation, an AUC of 0.4078 indicates that member and non-member loss distributions are substantially overlapping (corresponding to an AUC of 0.5922 under inverted positive polarity $s = +\mathcal{L}$), yielding an optimal classification accuracy of 51.00\% (near-random guessing). Because the MIA evaluation was performed on the shared base model representation of Client 0, both FedAvg and FedPer share the identical baseline representation evaluation on Client 0 (AUC 0.4078, 51.00\% accuracy), accounting for their identical entries in Table~\ref{tab:privacy_audit}. Note that DP-SGD perturbation was evaluated directly against gradient reconstruction (DLG), and its effect on membership inference was not separately measured; hence MIA is reported as not applicable (N/A) for DP-SGD rows in Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff}.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}
All experiments were executed on a standardized computing testbed equipped with a 14-core Intel processor, 32\,GB RAM, and PyTorch 2.x on Python 3.12. Optimization utilized the Adam optimizer with initial learning rate $\eta = 0.002$, mini-batch size $128$, weight decay $10^{-5}$, and gradient-norm clipping at 5.0. Federated benchmark runs executed for $R = 12$ communication rounds with $1$ local epoch per round ($E=1$), while centralized training and each isolated silo executed for 12 full epochs over their records, ensuring equal computational training budgets across all paradigms. To ensure statistical reliability and reproducibility, all benchmark models were evaluated across five repeated independent pseudo-random seeds (42, 43, 44, 45, 46), reporting multi-seed mean $\pm$ standard deviation for ADE and two-tailed paired $t$-test significance against FedPer.

Dataset partitioning strictly enforces vehicle-level disjoint splits: all continuous trajectory windows from a given vehicle (\texttt{Vehicle\_ID}) are assigned exclusively to either the training set (70\%), validation set (15\%), or test set (15\%) using a deterministic pseudo-random seed (seed 42), ensuring disjoint vehicle assignment between partitions. All five experimental evaluation seeds evaluate models on the exact invariant test window partitions (Client 0: 312 windows, Client 1: 1,680 windows, Client 2: 2,507 windows; 4,499 total test windows across 999,999 processed records).

We benchmark ten architectural paradigms and baselines:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Constant Velocity (CV):} Non-parametric kinematic extrapolation projecting the last observed velocity linearly over future elapsed time:
    \begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_t = p_0 + \tau_t v_0, \quad t \in \{1, \dots, T_{\mathrm{pred}}\},
    \label{eq:cv_baseline}
    \end{equation}
    where $\tau_t = t \Delta t$ denotes continuous prediction time ($\Delta t = 0.5$\,s at 2\,Hz downsampling), $p_0 \in \mathbb{R}^2$ is the vehicle coordinate at the current observation step ($t=0$, normalized to relative origin $p_0 = \mathbf{0}$ in the model frame), and $v_0 = (p_0 - p_{-1})/\Delta t$ is the velocity vector estimated from the backward finite difference of the final observed coordinates.
    \item \textbf{Constant Acceleration (CA):} Non-parametric kinematic extrapolation incorporating quadratic acceleration profiles:
    \begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_t = p_0 + \tau_t v_0 + \frac{1}{2} \tau_t^2 a_0, \quad t \in \{1, \dots, T_{\mathrm{pred}}\},
    \label{eq:ca_baseline}
    \end{equation}
    where $a_0 = (v_0 - v_{-1})/\Delta t = (p_0 - 2p_{-1} + p_{-2})/\Delta t^2$ is the acceleration vector estimated from second-order finite differences across the final three observed coordinates.
    \item \textbf{Centralized (Pooled):} Single global Seq2Seq LSTM trained on pooled multi-zone data for 12 epochs without explicit zone identifiers.
    \item \textbf{Centralized (Zone-Aware):} Seq2Seq LSTM equipped with shared backbone features and zone-specific projection heads trained for 12 epochs.
    \item \textbf{FedAvg (Global FL):} Canonical weighted parameter aggregation across all client RSUs~\cite{mcmahan2017communication}.
    \item \textbf{FedProx:} Regularized global aggregation with proximal coefficient $\mu = 0.01$~\cite{li2020federated}.
    \item \textbf{pFedMe:} Bi-level personalized optimization~\cite{dinh2020personalized} with Moreau parameter $\lambda = 1.0$, $K = 3$ inner steps, inner learning rate 0.01, and $\beta = 0.2$.
    \item \textbf{FedPer:} Decoupled representation backbone with private localized heads~\cite{arivazhagan2019federated}.
    \item \textbf{Isolated Silos:} Standalone models trained strictly within individual RSU boundaries (US-101 Silo, Lankershim Silo, Peachtree Silo), evaluated both in-zone and out-of-zone.
    \item \textbf{Isolated (Own Zone):} In-zone baseline where each RSU deploys its locally trained model exclusively within its native jurisdiction.
\end{enumerate}

Hyperparameters for FedProx ($\mu = 0.01$) and pFedMe ($\lambda = 1.0, K = 3, \beta = 0.2$) were adopted directly from standard literature recommendations~\cite{li2020federated,dinh2020personalized} without task-specific grid-search validation tuning on this three-zone dataset slice. Consequently, performance comparisons for FedProx and pFedMe are explicitly qualified as exploratory evaluations under default literature configurations, rather than definitive benchmarks of their peak capacity under exhaustive validation tuning. The observation and prediction horizons were configured to $T_{\mathrm{obs}} = 3.0$\,s and $T_{\mathrm{pred}} = 3.0$\,s (6 frames each at 2\,Hz), forming a symmetric 6.0\,s trajectory analysis window. While standard NGSIM benchmarks frequently evaluate a 3.0\,s observation / 5.0\,s prediction horizon (10 prediction frames), this study intentionally evaluated matched 3.0\,s horizons to evaluate symmetric encoder-decoder representations and consistent sequence dimensionalities; extending evaluation to full 5.0\,s horizons is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Model performance is evaluated across eight metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Average Displacement Error (ADE):} Mean Euclidean distance across all future timesteps:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathrm{ADE} = \frac{1}{N T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \|\hat{p}_t^{(i)} - p_t^{(i)}\|_2.
    \label{eq:ade}
    \end{equation}
    The horizon values ADE@1.0\,s and ADE@2.0\,s are cumulative: they average the error over all steps up to that horizon (steps 1--2 and 1--4, respectively), not the error at that instant.
    \item \textbf{Final Displacement Error (FDE):} Euclidean distance at final horizon $T_{\mathrm{pred}}$:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathrm{FDE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \|\hat{p}_{T_{\mathrm{pred}}}^{(i)} - p_{T_{\mathrm{pred}}}^{(i)}\|_2.
    \label{eq:fde}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):}
    \begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2 N T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \sum_{d=1}^2 (\hat{p}_{t, d}^{(i)} - p_{t, d}^{(i)})^2}.
    \label{eq:rmse}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE):}
    \begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{2 N T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \sum_{d=1}^2 |\hat{p}_{t, d}^{(i)} - p_{t, d}^{(i)}|.
    \label{eq:mae}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE):}
    \begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{2 N T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \sum_{d=1}^2 \frac{|\hat{p}_{t, d}^{(i)} - p_{t, d}^{(i)}|}{\max(|p_{t, d}^{(i)}|, \varepsilon)},
    \label{eq:mape}
    \end{equation}
    where $\varepsilon = 1.0$\,m avoids division singularity. Because future relative offsets can be near zero during stop-and-go arterial maneuvers, the 1.0\,m floor stabilizes computation but renders MAPE sensitive to near-origin coordinate drift.
    \item \textbf{Normalized Average Displacement Error (NADE):} To address this sensitivity with an interpretable scale-normalized metric, NADE normalizes ADE by the mean cumulative ground-truth trajectory path length over the prediction horizon, $\bar{L}_{\mathrm{path}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \|p_t^{(i)} - p_{t-1}^{(i)}\|_2$ (with $p_0^{(i)} = \mathbf{0}$):
    \begin{equation}
    \mathrm{NADE} = \frac{\mathrm{ADE}}{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{pred}}} \|p_t^{(i)} - p_{t-1}^{(i)}\|_2} \times 100\%.
    \label{eq:nade}
    \end{equation}
    Normalizing against the actual cumulative path length traversed rather than net linear displacement ensures that curvature, deceleration loops, and turning maneuvers across urban intersections are accurately captured without distortion.
    \item \textbf{Fairness Coefficient of Variation (CoV):} Spatial dispersion across zone errors $\mu_{\mathrm{zone}}$:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CoV} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{zone}}}{\mu_{\mathrm{zone}}} \times 100\%.
    \label{eq:cov}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Zone Disparity Gap ($\Delta_{\mathrm{ADE}}$):} Error spread between worst and best zones:
    \begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\mathrm{ADE}} = \max_k \mathrm{ADE}_k - \min_k \mathrm{ADE}_k.
    \label{eq:disparity_gap}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
A personalization recovery rate reported in an earlier version of this manuscript has been removed: it is undefined once a personalized model outperforms the centralized model, and it depended on unequal training budgets.

In terms of global displacement error, Centralized Pooled training with a matched budget of 12 epochs achieved a Global Pooled ADE of 0.835 $\pm$ 0.028\,m and FDE of 1.696\,m (11.5\% Normalized ADE). Centralized Zone-Aware training achieved 0.873 $\pm$ 0.055\,m ($p=0.2243$ vs. FedPer). In the federated regime over 12 communication rounds, canonical FedAvg achieved a Global Pooled ADE of 0.887 $\pm$ 0.048\,m (12.2\% NADE), while FedProx attained 0.941 $\pm$ 0.068\,m and FedPer attained 0.930 $\pm$ 0.058\,m ($p=0.2998$ vs. FedPer for FedAvg). With the exception of pFedMe (which diverged to 4.612\,m pooled ADE under uncalibrated default hyperparameters), evaluated deep learning approaches substantially outperformed kinematic physics baselines: Constant Velocity incurred a Global Pooled ADE of 0.990\,m (13.6\% NADE, $p=0.0808$ vs. FedPer), while Constant Acceleration degraded to 1.329\,m (18.3\% NADE, $p < 0.0001$). We explicitly note that comparisons for FedProx and pFedMe reflect default uncalibrated configurations rather than an optimized hyperparameter sweep on validation data.

When examining localized deployment at dedicated roadside units, the relevant baseline is a local model operating in its own home zone. Direct pooled evaluation across the exact test partitions (US-101: 312, Lankershim: 1,680, Peachtree: 2,507 windows; 4,499 windows total) demonstrated that isolated own-zone models achieved a pooled ADE of 0.880 $\pm$ 0.048\,m (0.998\,m on US-101, 0.720\,m on Lankershim, and 0.972\,m on Peachtree). FedPer reached 1.107\,m on US-101, 0.787\,m on Lankershim, and 1.004\,m on Peachtree. The paired $t$-test between isolated own-zone silos and FedPer yielded $p=0.2938$, indicating that the 0.050\,m difference is not statistically significant. While a lack of statistical significance does not establish statistical equivalence, it indicates that federation incurs no discernible accuracy penalty on native corridors while mitigating foreign-corridor transfer degradation.

Crucially, isolated data silos suffered substantial kinematic degradation when evaluated outside their home jurisdiction (Fig.~\ref{fig:cross_domain}). When deployed on Peachtree downtown traffic, the US-101 freeway silo experienced error compounding to 5.028 $\pm$ 0.139\,m ADE (a 5.04-fold error increase over home-zone performance, $p < 0.0001$). Similarly, the Lankershim arterial silo reached 1.217\,m on Peachtree. These empirical cross-domain penalties demonstrate that isolated edge sensing fails when encountering unfamiliar urban traffic topologies, supporting the utility of collaborative federated representation learning for robust cross-corridor transfer.

Regarding wireless communication consumption, FedAvg exchanged 37.24\,MiB and FedPer 36.65\,MiB total volume over 12 rounds (uplink plus downlink across three clients). A one-off upload of the four normalized float32 features across 999,999 records required 15.26\,MiB. This confirms that federated parameter exchange incurs comparable bandwidth to one-time feature aggregation while preserving raw telemetry decentralization on edge RSUs.

\subsection{Controlled Heterogeneity Resilience}
Table~\ref{tab:controlled_heterogeneity} and Fig.~\ref{fig:heterogeneity_regimes} report algorithmic performance under three resampled training partitions: Low ($\overline{\mathrm{JSD}} = 0.0524$), Medium ($\overline{\mathrm{JSD}} = 0.1088$), and High ($\overline{\mathrm{JSD}} = 0.1335$). Medium is the natural three-zone partition. Low moves 30\% of each zone's training windows into a shared pool and redistributes them across zones to synthesize inter-corridor overlap. High retains, in each zone, the 70\% of training windows whose kinematic profiles (mean velocity, acceleration, longitudinal displacement) deviate most from inter-zone centroids. Test sets remain completely invariant across all regimes.

This analysis evaluates early convergence dynamics over 3 communication rounds. As geographic non-IID divergence intensified from Low to High, FedAvg's unweighted mean zone ADE increased from 1.019\,m to 1.094\,m (+0.075\,m, a 7.4\% error inflation). FedPer's localized regression heads exhibited higher sensitivity during initial training rounds (increasing from 1.095\,m to 1.361\,m), because personal projection heads require sufficient local update epochs to calibrate facility-specific scale factors after backbone parameter aggregation. Over the complete 12-round training horizon reported in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}, however, FedPer converges fully to 0.930\,m, closely tracking FedAvg (0.887\,m).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The empirical results across five independent random seeds demonstrate that collaborative federated learning closely matches the predictive accuracy of centralized pooling while preserving edge data sovereignty. Over a matched budget of 12 epochs or rounds, Centralized Pooled training achieved a Global Pooled ADE of 0.835 $\pm$ 0.028\,m (statistically significantly outperforming FedPer with $p=0.0325$), while canonical FedAvg attained 0.887 $\pm$ 0.048\,m (exhibiting no statistically significant difference from FedPer with $p=0.2998$ on pooled ADE), and FedPer reached 0.930 $\pm$ 0.058\,m. With the exception of pFedMe (which diverged to 4.612\,m pooled ADE under uncalibrated literature default hyperparameters), evaluated deep sequential architectures substantially outperformed kinematic extrapolation baselines: Constant Velocity yielded a pooled ADE of 0.990\,m (13.6\% NADE, $p=0.0808$ vs. FedPer) and Constant Acceleration degraded to 1.329\,m (18.3\% NADE, $p < 0.0001$). Under contiguous trajectory assembly and standardized coordinates, FedAvg's shared coordinate projection benefited from aggregating temporal gradients across all three corridors, achieving slight global advantages over FedPer's locally specialized heads. Notably, FedProx ($\mu=0.01$) and pFedMe ($\lambda=1.0, K=3, \beta=0.2$) were evaluated under standard literature default hyperparameters without task-specific validation tuning, rendering these cross-method comparisons exploratory rather than a definitive ranking. Crucially, both federated formulations ensured robust cross-corridor generalization, mitigating the marked performance degradation observed when isolated models operate outside their training environments.

These findings clarify the operational roles of federated, centralized, and localized models in smart city traffic infrastructure. When evaluated within an RSU's dedicated home corridor, isolated models attained a pooled ADE of 0.880 $\pm$ 0.048\,m, with the paired $t$-test against FedPer (0.930\,m) yielding $p=0.2938$. While failure to reject the null hypothesis does not establish mathematical equivalence, it indicates that federation incurs no discernible accuracy penalty on native corridors while safeguarding against marked cross-zone performance drops. Standalone single-zone silos exhibited acute degradation when deployed on foreign corridors; most notably, the US-101 highway silo degraded by 5.04-fold to 5.028 $\pm$ 0.139\,m ADE on downtown Peachtree traffic ($p < 0.0001$). Collaborative federated learning is therefore valuable not to outperform well-trained isolated models on their familiar home terrain, but to ensure operational robustness and prevent transfer failures across heterogeneous urban topologies. Furthermore, our gradient inversion audit aligns with findings by Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu2019deep}: raw gradient exchanges allow accurate coordinate reconstruction (0.4373\,m error), and withholding output projection heads in FedPer provides negligible defense (0.2960\,m error). Effective privacy defense requires calibrated gradient perturbation, where empirical noise injection ($\sigma=1.0$) degrades single-window coordinate reconstruction to 5.1980\,m error, while separate formal batch DP-SGD moments accounting bounds the worst-case client privacy budget at $\epsilon \le 8.51$ ($\delta=10^{-5}$; per-client range $2.46$--$8.51$, and $\epsilon \le 2.80$ at $\sigma=2.0$).

From a practical implementation perspective, these results provide concrete guidance for municipal edge deployments. Over 12 training rounds across three RSUs, FedAvg exchanged 37.24\,MiB and FedPer exchanged 36.65\,MiB total volume, while a one-off upload of the four normalized float32 features across 999,999 records required 15.26\,MiB (excluding raw CSV metadata, identifiers, timestamps, and preprocessing overhead). Although federated learning does not yield immediate communication reductions relative to a single raw feature upload at this data scale, its primary justification lies in continuous edge data sovereignty, regulatory compliance, and resilience against centralized data breaches. For municipal agencies concerned with eavesdropping along wireless V2X transmission links, integrating DP-SGD provides formal differential privacy bounds (worst-case $\epsilon \le 8.51$ at $\sigma = 1.0$; client-level range $2.46$--$8.51$, and $\epsilon \le 2.80$ at $\sigma=2.0$), while empirical gradient perturbation ($\sigma \ge 1.0$) degrades single-window reconstruction fidelity beyond typical vehicle lane-width dimensions (3.0--3.7\,m), though downstream multi-object tracking resistance warrants task-specific empirical study.

Several practical limitations of this study should be recognized. First, the evaluation was conducted across three distinct urban corridors representing a three-zone transfer study; expanding to dozens of federated RSU clients across partitioned road segments remains important future work. Second, we evaluated a sequence-to-sequence recurrent architecture under a 3\,s observation / 3\,s prediction horizon; evaluating longer multi-modal forecasting horizons (e.g., 5\,s) under spatio-temporal graph attention represents a logical extension. Third, hyperparameters for FedProx ($\mu=0.01$) and pFedMe ($\lambda=1.0, K=3, \beta=0.2$) were adopted from standard literature defaults without client- or task-specific validation tuning, rendering these cross-method comparisons exploratory. Fourth, the differential privacy guarantee applies at the level of individual 3-second trajectory windows (window-level DP); deriving user-level or trip-level bounds across consecutive overlapping windows represents an open theoretical challenge. Finally, while the trajectory-assembly pipeline enforces frame contiguity and prevents cross-session track stitching, raw sensor tracking artifacts (such as the 14.00\,m / 0.5\,s displacement in Peachtree window 10712) remain preserved in the dataset to avoid artificial data sanitization. Furthermore, the NGSIM dataset is recorded under clear daylight conditions with fixed overhead cameras; performance under adverse weather, sensor noise, occlusions, and dynamic RSU disconnections requires dedicated field evaluation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This study formulated, benchmarked, and audited a federated vehicle trajectory forecasting framework across smart city roadside infrastructure under geographic non-IID kinematic distributions. By enforcing contiguous trajectory assembly and conducting multi-seed evaluation across matched training budgets, we demonstrated that federated learning achieves predictive accuracy (0.887\,m ADE for FedAvg, 0.930\,m for FedPer) closely matching centralized training (0.835\,m) and isolated own-zone baselines (0.880\,m), while outperforming kinematic physics baselines (0.990\,m CV, 1.329\,m CA). Crucially, federated representation sharing mitigates the transfer degradation of isolated edge silos, which suffer up to a 5.04-fold error increase (5.028\,m ADE) when deployed on unfamiliar corridors. Gradient inversion audits showed that withholding personalization heads alone does not prevent trajectory leakage (0.296\,m error), whereas empirical Gaussian perturbation ($\sigma=1.0$) degrades single-window coordinate reconstruction beyond standard lane widths (5.198\,m error), and separate batch DP-SGD moments accounting yields calibrated privacy bounds (worst-case $\epsilon \le 8.51$ at $\sigma=1.0$, per-client range $2.46$--$8.51$; $\epsilon \le 2.80$ at $\sigma=2.0$, $\delta=10^{-5}$). These findings establish quantitative empirical trade-offs between predictive accuracy, edge communication bandwidth, and differential privacy across heterogeneous smart city corridors.
